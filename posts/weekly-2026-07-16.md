## <i class="fa-solid fa-diagram-project"></i> One Stolen Token, 700 Companies Down

Microsoft's July 13 report maps a full year of ShinyHunters activity into three
repeatable techniques: vishing staff into approving rogue apps, abusing misconfigured
Salesforce guest access, and — the dangerous one — stealing OAuth tokens from trusted
SaaS vendors such as Drift and Gainsight. That vendor-integration route turned a single
compromised token into a cascade across hundreds of downstream tenants, with the group
claiming 1.5 billion records lifted from roughly 760 Salesforce instances. The named
victims read like a security-industry roll-call — Cloudflare, Zscaler, Palo Alto
Networks, Proofpoint, Tanium — sitting alongside earlier hits on Google, Qantas and
Allianz Life. This is a correlated-loss event dressed up as a series of unrelated
breaches.

> **Why it matters:** a shared SaaS integration is a single point of failure across your entire book — model the token, not the tenant.

## <i class="fa-solid fa-triangle-exclamation"></i> A Breach That Quadrupled in Slow Motion

Conduent's 2024–25 intrusion was quietly confirmed this month on the HHS breach portal
at 62,224,658 individuals — now the third-largest healthcare-linked breach in US
history. What makes it a case study is the trajectory, not just the size: the tally
climbed from 10.5 million last autumn, to 25.5 million in February, to 62.2 million now,
with the SafePay group claiming multiple terabytes including Social Security numbers,
medical records and Medicaid data. Notifications did not begin until roughly nine months
after detection, and the class actions are already stacking up. If you wrote this
govtech account, your reserve estimate has moved by a factor of six inside a year.

> **Why it matters:** cyber losses develop like casualty, not property — a claim you reserved at 10 million can be a 62-million-record liability twelve months on.

## <i class="fa-solid fa-plug-circle-xmark"></i> When the Insurer Is the Outage

TruStage — one of the largest providers of insurance and financial products sold through
US credit unions — pulled parts of its own network offline on July 15 after spotting
suspicious activity, activating incident-response before it knew the full picture. The
fallout landed immediately on its partners: credit unions including First Commonwealth
FCU told members that guaranteed-asset-protection, mechanical-repair and payment-
protection claims were temporarily unavailable. The company has not disclosed whether
ransomware was involved, when the intrusion began, or whether any data was accessed.
Even a clean, self-contained shutdown ripples through every institution that resold the
product.

> **Why it matters:** concentrate a product line behind one vendor and their incident becomes every downstream carrier's business-interruption claim.

## <i class="fa-solid fa-database"></i> The Policy Is Doing Its Job — Mostly

Willis's new *Cyber Claims in Focus* study, out July 13, is a rare dataset-backed reality
check on the perennial "coverage gap" narrative. Across 5,500 claims from 95 countries
and roughly $1 billion in insurer payments between 2013 and early 2026, it finds more
than 95% of average data-breach losses and 90% of average first-party losses are
adequately covered. The ransomware figures are the useful bit for pricing: a $5.3 million
mean loss, a largest single loss north of $500 million, average demands of $3.8 million
against typical payments of $1.5 million, and a 25-day average disruption. Reassuring on
the mean — but a distribution with a $500 million maximum is all about the tail the
average hides.

> **Why it matters:** when 95% of a loss is covered, the aggregate — not the retention — is where the insurer's real exposure sits.

[Read the source →](https://www.insurancejournal.com/magazines/mag-features/2026/07/13/877107.htm)

## <i class="fa-solid fa-flask"></i> Teaching Models to Imagine the Disaster They Haven't Seen

Not a cyber paper on its face, but squarely on point for anyone modelling cyber
catastrophe: Gupta and Deo's *An Extreme Value Perspective on Learning Stress Laws*
(arXiv, July 12) decomposes the tail of a distribution to generate representative extreme
scenarios and estimate probabilities *beyond* the observed data. That is precisely the
cyber-cat problem — a handful of historical accumulation events and a mandate to price
1-in-200 tails no one has ever witnessed. Techniques that synthesise credible extremes
from sparse history are exactly what cyber accumulation models are short of. Worth
watching from the methodology bench, even if the authors never mention insurance.

> **Why it matters:** cyber's loss history is too short to extrapolate from — the methodology to invent defensible tail scenarios is the whole game.

[Read the source →](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10700)
